# Stablecoins Enter the Regulatory Mainstream: Banks, CBDCs, and the Fight Over Digital Money

The most significant change in digital money is not simply that stablecoins are growing, but that they are being integrated into the regulated payments system. U.S. regulators are moving to define who may issue payment stablecoins, major banks are preparing their own token and settlement infrastructure, central banks are testing CBDC interoperability, and payment networks are expanding stablecoin acceptance. The result is a market in which stablecoins are no longer just a crypto asset class. They are becoming a contested layer of monetary infrastructure, with implications for bank deposits, government debt, cross-border payments, and financial stability.

## A U.S. rulebook is taking shape

The U.S. Treasury has proposed a rule implementing Section 3 of the GENIUS Act, focusing on which entities may issue, offer, sell, or otherwise make payment stablecoins available in the United States. The proposal defines key terms, limits U.S. issuance to permitted issuers unless specific safe harbors apply, and reaches certain activity outside the United States when payment stablecoins are offered or sold to persons located in the country. Separate FinCEN and OFAC proposals address BSA/AML/CFT and sanctions compliance.

The implementation schedule is now a central part of the debate. Comments are due by October 19, 2026, and regulators are targeting final rules by November 2026 after an earlier implementation milestone slipped. Restrictions on unlicensed stablecoin issuance are expected to begin in early 2027, while the GENIUS Act is set to take effect on the earlier of January 18, 2027, or 120 days after final regulations by the primary federal payment stablecoin regulators.

This is not just a licensing exercise. The proposed framework will determine how much of the payments stack can be owned by banks, fintechs, issuers, and global payment networks. It will also shape how quickly institutions can deploy stablecoin products without facing a patchwork of state rules.

## Short-term debt support, long-term limits

A key macroeconomic implication of the GENIUS Act framework is that permitted payment-stablecoin reserves are limited to cash-like assets and Treasuries with maturities of 93 days or less. That constraint means stablecoin growth can add demand for short-term U.S. government funding, but it does not create a direct source of demand for longer-dated bonds.

Treasury has separately responded to long-end liquidity concerns by planning to at least double the maximum size of liquidity-support buybacks in the 10- to 20-year and 20- to 30-year sectors. The move would raise aggregate buyback capacity across the affected operations from $14 billion to at least $28 billion and targets older nominal bonds. In effect, stablecoins may help the short end of the curve, but the long-end liquidity program remains a separate Treasury tool.

## Banks are building their own digital-dollar stack

Major financial institutions are moving from experimentation to product development. JPMorgan Chase has had early internal discussions about issuing its own public-facing stablecoin, though it has no active issuance plans and says its existing JPM Coin remains a tokenized deposit product aimed at institutional clients.

Other banks are pursuing shared infrastructure. Wells Fargo, Bank of America, and other lenders are advancing a jointly sponsored digital token for commercial use, initially dollar-backed, with possible future versions in other Group of Seven currencies. JPMorgan, Bank of America, Citigroup, and other Clearing House members are building a shared network for tokenized commercial-bank deposits aimed at 24/7 interbank settlement, with a launch targeted for the first half of 2027.

The BankChain Alliance, formed by 39 state banking associations, is working on a shared permissioned blockchain for tokenized deposits, stablecoins, and programmable payments. Zelle's bank-owned operator has launched the ZLUSD stablecoin, adding another bank-owned entry point into digital dollar payments.

Japan's largest banking groups are moving in a similar direction. Mitsubishi UFJ, Mizuho, and Sumitomo Mitsui plan to conduct commercial stablecoin transactions by March 2027 to retain deposits and payment activity from nonbank issuers. The banks have formed a voluntary council to develop governance and operating standards and will act as joint settlement institutions. Initial use cases include cross-border payments, digital-asset settlement, and programmable payments, with a focus on corporate clients.

## The yield fight is testing community-bank support

The most politically charged issue is whether stablecoin issuers can offer rewards or yield to users. American community banks are demanding a complete ban on stablecoin rewards, arguing that allowing yields could trigger large deposit outflows and reduce local lending. The Independent Community Bankers of America has warned that up to $1.3 trillion in deposits could move to stablecoins, potentially cutting local loans by $850 billion.

That concern has intensified opposition to the CLARITY Act and complicated Senate progress. Some lawmakers have joined bank concerns, while a compromise limiting certain rewards remains contested. In states such as Missouri, the argument is less about blocking innovation and more about giving banks a clear rulebook rather than adding new regulatory roadblocks. One Missouri community bank has already settled stablecoin transactions nationwide, suggesting that early digital-dollar infrastructure can expand banking activity rather than replace it.

The debate matters because stablecoin rewards sit at the intersection of payments, deposit competition, and monetary policy. If rewards are permitted, stablecoins become a direct competitor to bank deposits. If they are restricted, stablecoins may remain useful for payments but less attractive as a place to hold idle balances.

## CBDC diplomacy and local-currency strategies

Central banks are not waiting for private stablecoins to define the future. India is preparing to pitch a BRICS central bank digital currency at the September 12-13 summit in New Delhi. The plan would link member central banks' digital currencies for cross-border trade and settlements, potentially reducing conversion costs and dependence on external payment systems, including the U.S. dollar. Finance ministers and central bank governors are expected to discuss implementation in early September.

Delhi's Lakshmi Yojana offers a more granular domestic example. Under the scheme, Rs 1,500 of each eligible woman's Rs 2,500 monthly benefit will be placed in a recurring deposit locked until July 31, 2029, while Rs 1,000 will be credited to a CBDC wallet linked to the recipient's bank account. Beneficiaries may instead deposit the full amount into a recurring deposit. The CBDC portion is restricted from use for alcohol, tobacco, narcotics, psychotropic substances, lottery tickets, gambling, or betting. The government says the Council of Ministers can review or change the maturity period two years after the scheme begins.

Europe is developing parallel paths. The ECB is designing a digital euro with strong privacy protections, while private firms launch euro stablecoins. Revolut is rolling out EURR, an Ethereum-based stablecoin issued by Stripe subsidiary Bridge Building and pegged to one euro, to users in Denmark, Poland, and Portugal, with plans to expand across the European Economic Area. The competition will focus on privacy, usability, and regulatory clarity, with the digital euro remaining public central-bank money and private stablecoins serving a different role.

## Emerging-market payments: Argentina, Russia, and Korea

Argentina shows why stablecoins can become a durable payments habit rather than a temporary hedge. One in five Argentines uses crypto, and the country's long-standing preference for dollars, combined with periods of currency controls, has driven strong adoption of dollar-pegged stablecoins as savings and payment tools. Stablecoins now make up about 94% of peso crypto trading. Their use in contractor pay rose during high inflation before stabilizing as inflation cooled and official dollar access improved, but wallet downloads continue to climb.

Russia presents a more constrained case. Sberbank, the country's largest bank, says it plans to accept Bitcoin, Ether, and Tether's USDT as loan collateral once the central bank permits public circulation. The Bank of Russia has proposed Bitcoin, Ether, and USDT for regulated exchange trading based on market-capitalization, volume, and price-history requirements. Russia's new digital-currency law continues to ban domestic crypto payments, with a carve-out only for foreign trade. Sberbank has not disclosed loan terms or a launch date, but it has previously run a crypto-backed lending pilot and is developing a digital depository. The bank also said it sees little clear demand for the digital ruble among retail customers, corporate clients, or financial institutions ahead of a wider rollout.

Korea offers a complementary angle. Hecto Financial is projected to post record annual sales of 221.3 billion won and operating profit of 25.6 billion won as Korea's digital-asset legislation, including stablecoin rules, moves closer to implementation. The company is an official partner in Circle's Circle Payments Network and says it can link won stablecoin wallets, issuance, and settlement functions to the network's global payment infrastructure. It is also targeting AI-agent payments and high-frequency cross-border settlement using stablecoins and deposit tokens through the x402 ecosystem.

## Consumer spending and liquidity are scaling

The private stablecoin market is now large enough to influence payment flows. USDT's market capitalization has reached approximately $183.4 billion, while USDC stands near $74 billion, together representing more than $257 billion in dollar-linked crypto liquidity. Circle's agreement to place USDC branding on Chelsea FC shirts for the 2026/27 season adds mainstream visibility to the second-largest stablecoin.

Card payments are the clearest evidence of real-world usage. Cumulative stablecoin card spending has surpassed $10.9 billion, and July 2026 became the first month in which stablecoin card payments exceeded $1 billion. Dollar-backed stablecoins dominated usage, led by USDC and USDT, while network expansion through Visa and Mastercard is allowing merchants to accept payments without handling crypto directly. RedotPay forecasts annualized stablecoin-card spending will grow from roughly $12.5 billion to $50 billion by 2028. That would still be small compared with traditional card payments, but the trajectory suggests a meaningful consumer channel.

Retail attention is also extending beyond the largest issuers, as smaller digital-asset projects draw presale interest and investors seek exposure to new stablecoin-adjacent opportunities. Stablecoin exchange flows have shifted from net outflows to net inflows for the first time in three months, coinciding with a Bitcoin rebound. That is not a guarantee of sustained market strength, but it is a useful signal that digital-dollar liquidity may be supporting risk assets as stablecoins become a broader settlement layer.

## Stablecoins versus tokenized deposits

The deepest disagreement is over what should become the default digital-money system: fully reserved stablecoins or tokenized bank deposits. Tether CEO Paolo Ardoino has argued that the Bank for International Settlements is rightly concerned that stablecoins expose the fractional-reserve weakness in traditional banking, but that fully reserved stablecoins backed by liquid assets are structurally safer. He has described tokenized bank deposits as often uninsured and supported by only about 10% in liquid reserves, questioning why savers would choose fractional-reserve products if they understood the difference.

BIS General Manager Pablo Hernández de Cos has pushed back, saying tokenized bank deposits, not stablecoins, are the more promising foundation for future money. At the Jackson Hole Economic Policy Symposium, he argued that stablecoins still fail as credible money at scale because they lack par redeemability, elasticity, interoperability, and financial integrity. He urged central banks to prioritize tokenized deposits, which remain bank liabilities and settle through central bank reserves, as the main vehicle for everyday payments.

The debate is not academic. Global stablecoin supply has reached about $308 billion, raising concerns about monetary sovereignty and digital dollarization. A BIS-linked study found that five major jurisdictions use different rules for stablecoin issuance and related activities, while the U.S. GENIUS Act requires payment stablecoins to hold one-for-one short-term reserve assets. If stablecoins gain scale, they could raise bank funding costs or create run risk. If tokenized deposits dominate, banks and central banks retain more control over monetary policy transmission, but the system remains tied to bank balance sheets.

## What it means

Stablecoins are moving from a peripheral crypto market into the center of payments and financial infrastructure. The U.S. is setting the issuer rules, banks are building competing rails, central banks are testing CBDC alternatives, and emerging markets are using digital money to address currency instability, deposit competition, and cross-border settlement costs.

The next phase will depend on three questions: whether stablecoin rewards are allowed, whether tokenized deposits or stablecoins become the default digital payment layer, and whether CBDC interoperability can reduce reliance on the dollar without recreating financial fragmentation. The coming year will likely determine which institutions control the rails, who can earn the fees, and how much of the payments system remains bank-based, state-backed, or privately issued.
