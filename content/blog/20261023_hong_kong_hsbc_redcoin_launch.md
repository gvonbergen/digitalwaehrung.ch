## Hong Kong: HSBC RedCoin Launch

HSBC launched HSBC RedCoin (RCHKD), its first Hong Kong dollar-pegged stablecoin, on the PayMe payment app. Licensed by the Hong Kong Monetary Authority, the coin gives more than 3.3 million users instant digital payments to friends, family, and over 100,000 merchants. The bank plans to expand utility by integrating RedCoin into its mobile banking app, enabling subscriptions to tokenized investment products, and extending services to wholesale corporate clients. The launch shows licensed stablecoins moving beyond crypto trading into everyday retail payments in a major Asian financial hub.

## Hong Kong: Stablecoins Ordinance

Hong Kong implemented a comprehensive Stablecoins Ordinance in August 2025, creating a licensing regime administered by the Hong Kong Monetary Authority. Licensed issuers must meet strict reserve requirements, segregate assets, and provide redemption rights, strengthening consumer protection and reserve transparency. The framework has begun issuing licenses, positioning Hong Kong as a regulated stablecoin hub. These rules support bank-issued tokens such as HSBC RedCoin and provide a template for other jurisdictions seeking to balance innovation with financial stability. The ordinance also clarifies expectations for foreign issuers operating into Hong Kong.

## Philippines: Dual Regulation, Moratorium, and Peso Stablecoins

The Philippines regulates stablecoins through a dual framework, with the Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas overseeing virtual asset service providers and the Securities and Exchange Commission treating tokens as securities when structured as investment contracts. New VASP licenses have been suspended under an indefinite moratorium since 2022, forcing prospective issuers into the central bank’s regulatory sandbox. Only one peso-pegged stablecoin has graduated, PHPC, while domestic banks and payment providers develop peso-backed stablecoins and settlement systems. Pending legislation aims to create a dedicated statutory framework for tokenization and stablecoins, resolving current barriers.

## SoFi Earn: FDIC-Insured Tokenized Deposits

SoFi launched SoFi Earn (SOFIE), claiming the first consumer retail bank-issued, U.S. national-bank tokenized deposit in the United States. Each SOFIE token represents $1 in an FDIC-insured SoFi Bank deposit account, covered up to $250,000, pays a variable 4.40% annual percentage yield continuously, and yields above the Fed funds rate. Users can convert SOFIE one-for-one into the non-insured SoFiUSD stablecoin for spending, external transfers, and cryptocurrency purchases on blockchain rails. The rollout, targeted at SoFi Crypto members through November, builds on prior card-settlement work and about $300 million of circulating SoFiUSD as of Q2. Launched hours after the OCC finalized GENIUS Act rules banning issuer-paid stablecoin yield, SOFIE positions tokenized deposits as an interest-bearing, bank-protected alternative that helps banks retain deposits amid competition from idle stablecoins and tokenized money-market funds.

## U.S.: OCC Finalizes GENIUS Act Stablecoin Rules

The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency finalized GENIUS Act rules implementing the first federal licensing framework for bank-issued stablecoins, effective January 18, 2027. The rule sets federal licensing, supervision, reserve, redemption, custody, and 1:1 backing standards for eligible nonbank financial companies and foreign issuers. It keeps the statutory ban on issuer-paid yield, removes draft rebuttable presumption treating third-party rewards as circumvention, and permits case-by-case review of exchange rewards not equivalent to bank deposit interest, such as liquidity-pool participation. Issuers gain reserve flexibility, including longer-dated assets. Banking groups say ambiguity undermines Congress’s intent to protect local lending and risks draining $850 billion from community banks; crypto firms say it aligns with statute. Licensing applications remain closed and anti-money-laundering rules are unfinished; EU MiCAR still bans interest on e-money tokens.

## U.S.: State Regulators Seek Safe Harbors

State banking regulators urged the U.S. Treasury to create safe harbors for state-licensed stablecoin issuers under the GENIUS Act, warning that absent federal protection issuers will migrate to the federal Office of the Comptroller of the Currency pathway. The New York Department of Financial Services opposes the request, arguing existing statutes already allow continued operations and that Treasury’s proposed rules are too lenient toward foreign stablecoins. The dispute highlights regulatory divergence as the January 18 effective date approaches and certification deadlines have been missed. The outcome may determine whether stablecoin issuance concentrates federally or preserves state-level competition and oversight.

## TRON: Q3 Stablecoin Settlement Volumes

TRON processed $2.21 trillion in stablecoin settlements during Q3 2026, a 5.1% increase from the prior quarter, across 1.07 billion successful transactions. Binance and OKX led labeled activity, while the settlement data did not break out merchant payments from exchange-related transfers. The network’s combined USDT and USDD supply reached $93.88 billion at quarter-end. The report lacks a direct volume comparison with Ethereum and Solana for the same period, limiting assessment of TRON’s relative share, but the figures confirm its continuing role as a major rail for stablecoin transfers and settlement.

## MoonPay and Dunamu: Exploratory GIWA Collaboration

MoonPay and Dunamu, operator of South Korea’s Upbit exchange, announced an exploratory strategic collaboration focused on stablecoin-based cross-border payments, corporate treasury management, and global expansion of Dunamu’s GIWA blockchain. The partnership aims to use MoonPay’s extensive user network to distribute GIWA-based services internationally, although the Ethereum layer-2 chain has not launched its mainnet. Because the agreement is preliminary, no specific commercial terms, product launches, or timelines were disclosed. The collaboration signals growing interest in combining exchange liquidity, payment infrastructure, and blockchain rails for cross-border financial services.

## Sweden: Revived e-Krona Debate

Sweden’s central bank revived the debate over introducing a retail central bank digital currency, the e-krona, citing the imminent launch of the digital euro and the growing influence of stablecoins as threats to the krona’s competitiveness. The Riksbank warns that without a usable digital form of the currency, Sweden risks losing monetary policy transmission and financial stability, especially because the country is highly cashless. Deputy Governor Per Jansson urged parliament to begin legislative preparations immediately, noting implementation would take at least five years to prevent Sweden from falling behind in the evolving digital payment landscape.

## Corporate Adoption: Stablecoins Move Into Production

A 2026 EY-Parthenon survey indicates stablecoin adoption is moving from experimentation to implementation, with 18% of corporates using them in production and 69% of financial institutions offering or planning to launch services. Cross-border payments remain the primary use case, while the market is increasingly multi-rail, with 45% of corporates favoring coexistence of stablecoins and tokenized deposits to combine reach and programmability. Successful scaling will depend on integrating digital assets into existing enterprise workflows and banking interfaces, alongside development of programmable payment capabilities. The findings suggest stablecoins are entering mainstream corporate finance, not just crypto-native niches.

## Remittances: Stablecoins Disrupt Global Money Transfer

A&M research shows rapid stablecoin adoption is disrupting the global remittance industry and challenging traditional money-transfer providers. Global stablecoin payments reached at least $401 billion in the first eight months of 2026, a 42% year-over-year increase. The growth is pressuring traditional fee structures and compelling established financial institutions to invest in their own digital payment infrastructures to remain competitive. The findings position stablecoins as a practical remittance channel, with A&M Managing Director Kumanan Ramanathan discussing how the technology is reshaping cross-border money movement and forcing incumbents to respond.

## Philippines: Low Trust in Stablecoin Remittances

A Visa survey reveals awareness and trust in stablecoins as a remittance tool remain low among Filipinos, with only 40% having heard of them and just 9% expressing strong trust for transfers. The study highlights significant knowledge gaps and misconceptions, noting government and central banks are the most trusted providers for digital currency initiatives. Despite skepticism, Visa is exploring stablecoin pilots to address remittance pain points such as high fees, aiming to drive adoption through educational efforts and improved digital payment infrastructure. The results show regulatory clarity and public trust are prerequisites for mass-market stablecoin remittances.

## South Africa: Treasury Rewrites Crypto Regulations

South Africa’s rand stablecoin issuers welcomed Finance Minister Enoch Godongwana’s announcement that Treasury will rewrite draft crypto regulations to assess transactions for legitimacy and tax compliance, moving away from pre-approval regimes. Industry leaders argue the changes should explicitly classify stablecoin payments as domestic transactions rather than capital flows, allowing businesses to use them for cross-border trade without regulatory barriers. The revised approach, guided by Financial Stability Board recommendations, aims to foster a transparent local payment ecosystem while preventing the rand from becoming peripheral in the global digital asset market. The shift could unlock enterprise stablecoin use if tax and capital-control treatment is clarified.

## Payments Networks: Scaling Stablecoin Rails

Global stablecoin transfer volume has surged above $400 million per hour, with 73% of transactions occurring outside U.S. banking hours because blockchain rails operate continuously. Mastercard launched new infrastructure products, including the Global Money Platform and AI-focused Agent Pay for Machines, while Visa advanced its Direct stablecoin payouts to commercial deployment and partnered with Mesh to enable direct crypto spending at merchants. Meanwhile, the Trump-linked USD1 stablecoin experienced a roughly 10% decline in market cap, contrasting with the relative stability of major competitors USDT and USDC. The developments show payments networks scaling stablecoin rails, with competition and trust shaping market share.

## Banking Infrastructure: Digital Money and AI Agents

FIS executive Jon Eisenstein argues digital money, including tokenized deposits and stablecoins, forces banks to make critical infrastructure decisions around institutional control, network interoperability, and real-time settlement controls. He notes tokenized deposits suit existing banking relationships, while stablecoins offer broader distribution across networks, requiring banks to adapt funding, reconciliation, and audit processes for faster transaction speeds. The rise of artificial-intelligence agents initiating financial transactions also demands new governance frameworks for permissions and delegated authority. These themes highlight the convergence of decentralized and traditional finance and the operational risks banks face as digital money moves into core banking.

## Adoption: Ubiquity Over Yield

An analysis argues stablecoins need ubiquity more than yield. The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency finalized rule bans direct interest payments to customers but permits rewards arrangements case by case, likely prompting issuers to develop yield-like mechanisms that circumvent the ban. Despite this regulatory flexibility, stablecoins face a critical adoption barrier because they lack universal acceptance outside stagnant crypto markets. The argument holds that achieving ubiquity is far more essential for stablecoin success than offering yield, because currency utility depends on widespread legal and commercial acceptance, not marginal return incentives.

## Outlook

Stablecoin regulation is shifting from sandbox experimentation to formal licensing, with federal, state, and international frameworks competing for issuers. Bank-issued tokenized deposits are emerging as interest-bearing complements to stablecoins, while payments networks and corporates expand real-world use. Adoption will depend less on yield than on acceptance, trust, interoperability, and clear supervisory treatment, especially as AI-driven payments and cross-border demand accelerate.
