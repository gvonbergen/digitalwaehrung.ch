## Regulatory and Central-Bank Developments

Bank of Japan Executive Director Kazushige Kamiyama told an IMF seminar that stablecoins need strong regulation of reserve assets, structural frameworks, and reliable links to central bank money. He warned that weak oversight and poor interoperability among multiple digital currencies could fragment financial markets and undermine settlement finality. While noting distributed ledger technology improves transaction efficiency, he stressed risks from greater institutional interconnectedness, volatility amplified by automated settlements, and liquidity fragmentation. His comments underscore growing central-bank concern that privately issued stablecoins must remain anchored in the monetary system to preserve trust in payments.

Pablo Hernández de Cos, the current Bank for International Settlements general manager, is set to become the next European Central Bank president, succeeding Christine Lagarde. A noted critic of stablecoins, he has argued they are not credible for widespread payments and advocates tokenized bank deposits and central bank digital currencies to protect monetary sovereignty. His appointment positions him to lead the ECB’s digital euro project, which includes a planned pilot in 2027 and a potential launch in 2029. The transition signals a more assertive push for public digital money in Europe.

The Bank of Israel shelved plans for a retail digital shekel, concluding that current indicators do not support an immediate issuance decision. Instead, it will launch a two-year pilot for a wholesale CBDC focused on tokenized institutional money, especially settlement of tokenized treasury bills. The shift moves the debate from the general public to the institutional financial system, allowing the central bank to examine tokenization and wholesale money with financial-sector stakeholders. The decision reflects a phased approach, prioritizing market infrastructure and settlement efficiency before retail adoption.

South Korea’s Financial Supervisory Service has been excluded from the newly proposed Committee on Digital Payment Tokens, which will advise on stablecoin issuance volumes and reserve-asset composition under the Digital Asset Framework Act. The committee is chaired by the Financial Services Commission vice chairman and includes the Ministry of Economy and Finance and the Bank of Korea, but omits the FSS despite its statutory role examining and supervising stablecoin issuers. Critics say the exclusion weakens oversight because the FSS argued direct participation is essential to assess stablecoins’ impact on monetary and foreign-exchange policy. The episode highlights jurisdictional tensions in Korea’s digital-asset governance.

South Korea’s Financial Services Commission published draft regulations allowing domestic banks to launch stablecoins through consortiums in which local banks hold a majority stake. The framework also proposes easing the nine-year ban on initial coin offerings by requiring issuers to file detailed white papers with the regulator. It introduces ownership limits for shareholders of digital-asset exchanges and disclosure requirements intended to improve market transparency. The proposals mark a shift toward supervised, bank-led stablecoin issuance while opening capital markets to token offerings under stricter conditions.

As of May 2026, stablecoins held $320 billion market capitalization under private issuer liability, while only three countries had fully launched central bank digital currencies and 146 were exploring them. The core distinction is backing: stablecoins require 1-to-1 reserves under laws like the US GENIUS Act and EU MiCA, which bar interest payments, whereas CBDCs are direct central bank liabilities. Regulatory paths diverge sharply. The US prohibits CBDC development through Executive Order 14178, the EU targets digital euro issuance by 2029, and China requires banks to pay interest on digital yuan balances. The contrast shows competition over settlement infrastructure, privacy, and monetary control.

## Stablecoin Regulation and Market Structure

The OCC’s final rule under the GENIUS Act preserves the statutory ban on stablecoin issuers paying interest, but removes a proposed presumption that third-party rewards, including exchange incentives, are automatically prohibited yield. The change shifts reward programs from a presumptive bar to case-by-case assessment based on economic substance, creating regulatory uncertainty for platforms such as Coinbase. Banking groups criticized the shift as increasing deposit-substitution risk. The rule also sets new reserve and redemption standards for payment stablecoins, effective January 18, 2027, and signals future rulemaking to clarify the boundary between permitted loyalty rewards and banned yield.

The GENIUS Act creates separate regulatory timelines for stablecoin issuance and distribution, with key restrictions on foreign-issued tokens taking effect in January 2027 and July 2028. Foreign issuers seeking access must obtain Treasury determinations that their home regimes are comparable, register with the OCC, and maintain sufficient reserves at U.S. financial institutions to support redemptions. The framework forces businesses to conduct separate due diligence on tokens such as USDT and USAT, because compliance for one product does not guarantee eligibility for the other. The result is a more fragmented market-entry map for cross-border stablecoin providers.

The European Banking Authority warned that decentralized finance poses growing risks to investors and to regulated stablecoins. It noted that access barriers are falling through crypto exchanges, wallet integrations, and AI agents, potentially accelerating adoption even though current engagement remains limited. The EBA emphasized that MiCA-regulated stablecoins, including USDC, are already deeply embedded in DeFi lending, while euro stablecoins such as Societe Generale FORGE and Monerium’s EURe have significant portions locked in DeFi protocols. These exposures raise concerns about contagion, operational failures, and investor harm if lending markets become more interconnected.

The EBA recommended that the European Commission evaluate measures restricting decentralized finance platforms from facilitating lending with stablecoins that lack MiCA authorization. The proposal aims to stop intermediaries from helping users circumvent MiCA’s ban on paying interest for e-money tokens, reducing risks from defaults and operational failures in DeFi lending. If adopted, European platforms may prioritize authorized stablecoins such as USDC and EURC over unauthorized tokens like USDT. The move would strengthen the EU stablecoin perimeter, but could limit user access relative to other global markets where unlicensed tokens remain widely available.

USD stablecoin supply contracted by $20.7 billion in 2026, the first annual decline since 2023, with capitalization near $280-$282 billion. November removed $14.8 billion, the steepest monthly fall since May 2022, while $12 billion net redemptions over 30 days cut USDT and USDC supplies by 4%. During Bitcoin’s November sell-off, $10.7 billion vanished over 18 days, the largest decline since Terra, amplified by the Binance hack and $1.6 billion of forced long liquidations. Since May, about $30 billion exited dollar pegs, including a 50% collapse in Sky’s USDS. Nearly 90% occurred on Ethereum, showing capital leaving crypto. Weak ETF inflows and tighter Fed policy halved USDT volume from its 2025 average, making recoveries fragile, though some view the contraction as a bear-market bottom signal and say issuance must expand again.

## Stablecoin Adoption and Payments Infrastructure

HSBC’s Hong Kong dollar-denominated stablecoin, HSBC RedCoin, surpassed 100,000 users about one month after launch, indicating rapid customer uptake. The product enables instant transfers and payments through PayMe at more than 100,000 merchants, and eligible customers can access a tokenized unit class of a Hong Kong dollar money market fund via the HSBC HK Mobile Banking app. HSBC said future expansion into broader wealth-management and wholesale use cases will proceed in stages, guided by regulatory requirements, robust reserves, and strong financial-crime controls. The rollout highlights banks testing regulated stablecoin rails inside existing retail ecosystems.

OCBC Bank and StraitsX launched a pilot integrating stablecoin-based settlement with OCBC’s banking services to streamline cross-border business-to-business payments. StraitsX’s Dedicated Wallet Address infrastructure manages on-chain compliance and reconciliation in the background, while OCBC converts stablecoins into fiat so funds arrive directly in standard bank accounts. The model lets companies use stablecoin speed without adopting new digital wallets or changing financial operations. Initial participant NTT DOCOMO ASIA operates under the Monetary Authority of Singapore’s BLOOM initiative, linking digital-asset settlement with conventional banking relationships.

Sentvia expanded its Prime OTC desk to serve business clients across all eligible industries, enabling conversions between stablecoins such as USDT, USDC, and EURC and traditional currencies including USD, EUR, GBP, and AED. The service functions as a hybrid settlement model, allowing businesses to use stablecoin speed for transfers while settling into standard bank accounts through SWIFT, SEPA, and other payment rails. The expansion follows success with luxury-goods dealers and financial brokers, offering a compliant bridge between digital assets and fiat for treasury, payroll, and supplier payments.

Ripple’s RLUSD is a fiat-backed stablecoin issued on the XRP Ledger and Ethereum, designed primarily for enterprise payments, settlement, and treasury flows. It is backed 1-to-1 by cash, U.S. Treasuries, and government money market funds, with reserves exceeding circulating supply in late 2026. The token has regulatory approvals from New York’s Department of Financial Services, Japan’s JFSA, and MiCA authorization across the EEA, positioning it as a compliant alternative to USDC and USDT. Institutions can mint and redeem RLUSD directly via Ripple Mint, while retail users typically access it through major cryptocurrency exchanges.

Emirates Draw, a global lottery platform, integrated cryptocurrency payments by enabling eligible users to deposit and withdraw winnings using USDT and USDC stablecoins. The feature creates a two-way payment rail for players to transact in U.S.-dollar-pegged tokens, mitigating exposure to large price swings. The company plans to expand supported token offerings later, while maintaining specific withdrawal limits and compliance requirements. The rollout illustrates consumer-facing crypto payment adoption in regulated gaming, using stablecoins to reduce volatility and simplify cross-border transactions.

Stablecoins are gaining traction in emerging markets by addressing everyday payments, currency devaluation, and costly international transfers. Research indicates that a significant majority of consumers in these regions would consider using stablecoins for routine expenses such as groceries and rent to protect purchasing power and reduce cross-border fees. Adoption is driven by immediate financial pressures rather than speculative investment, suggesting merchant integration and seamless checkout experiences are key to broader acceptance. The trend positions stablecoins as practical financial tools where local currencies and remittance channels are under stress.

A Neo survey found that 88% of senior finance professionals at internationally active small and medium-sized enterprises believe stablecoins could improve cross-border business-to-business collections, yet only 27% currently accept them. Barriers include operational complexity and regulatory uncertainty, with many firms preferring automatic stablecoin-to-fiat conversion to avoid managing digital assets directly. Although B2B stablecoin transactions are growing, they remain a small fraction of global payments. The findings show stablecoins are viewed as a complementary option to existing currency rails rather than a full replacement, with adoption dependent on simpler workflows and clearer rules.

An analysis of paying international creators compared traditional wires, stablecoins, and payout platforms such as Wise, PayPal, Slash, and Lumanu. Stablecoin payouts, including USDC via Slash, offer an alternative to SWIFT wires by letting brands send digital assets from dollar balances, with fees varying by volume and no immediate currency conversion. The analysis notes that stablecoins avoid sender-side exchange spreads, but recipients must manage conversion through their own exchanges and lack FDIC insurance. The comparison underscores cost advantages alongside custody, compliance, and consumer-protection trade-offs.

## Tokenization and Stablecoin Integration

Tokenized stocks reached approximately $3.6 billion on public blockchains by October 2026, driven by stablecoin integration and regulatory developments such as the SEC’s Innovation Exemption. Most assets are 1-to-1 backed certificates giving economic exposure and dividends without voting rights, enabling non-U.S. investors to trade U.S. shares 24/7 through self-custody wallets. Decentralized exchange liquidity remains limited, so specialized protocols facilitate larger trades by matching user intents with KYB-verified solvers that mint tokens directly with issuers. The growth shows stablecoins acting as settlement rails for tokenized equities, expanding access while raising questions about investor protection and issuer responsibility.

## Overall Outlook

The digital-currency landscape is moving toward regulated stablecoins, tokenized bank deposits, and selective CBDC pilots, with central banks prioritizing settlement safety, reserve quality, and monetary sovereignty. Stablecoin liquidity is shrinking, adoption is expanding through banks and enterprise rails, and tokenization is deepening integration with capital markets. Competition will intensify as jurisdictions align rules, launch pilots, and compete for cross-border settlement leadership.
